# RESEARCH — Voices of the War: Resistance Poetry & Song Corpus (1902–1934)
Research dossier for the quotes strip ("Voices of the War") and the "The Poets' War" section (EN/FR/AR).
Compiled 2026-11-01. Research-only; no verse invented anywhere in this file.

## Tag legend
- **[VERIFIED source]** — text traced to a named print/online source with author/authority.
- **[ATTRIBUTED-traditional, unverified]** — verse attributed to a named poet by tradition / a single compiled secondary source; wording may vary between transmitters.
- **[ANACHRONISM RISK]** — authentic but composed outside 1902–1934; use only in a clearly dated "later echo" frame, never as a 1902–34 voice.

## Translation caveat (read before using anything)
All French/English renderings below are **working translations by the project**, made for site mockups.
No published scholarly translation of these Hassaniya texts was found online (Norris 1968 and Bah translate
other pieces; those are cited where used). Before shipping EN/FR, have a Hassaniya speaker check every line.

## SECTION 1 — Hassaniya resistance verse (l-ġnā / كاف kāf) under the French occupation
**Corpus source:** «أدب المقاومة في عهد الاحتلال الفرنسي لبلاد موريتانيا — الشق الشعبي (لغن)»
("Resistance literature under the French occupation of Mauritania — the popular/sung part (l-ġnā)"),
compiled study presenting **23 numbered texts with named poets and historical context**,
published 26 July 2018 on rimnow.net (Mauritanian news/culture site; author uncredited on page).
URL: https://www.rimnow.net/w/?q=ar/node/8970 — local copy: `rts_research/pages/rimnow_8970.txt`
Unless a second source is named, every item in this section is **[ATTRIBUTED-traditional, unverified — single compiled source: rimnow.net 2018]**.
The poets (Emir Sidi Ahmed ould Ahmeddou, Cheikh Saad Bouh, the Kounti shaykhs) are all
independently documented historical figures; the *verse wording* is what tradition carries.

### 1.1 — Mohamed El Mokhtar ould El Hamed ould Aminouh (al-Kounti) mocks Coppolani
Kounti shaykh and resistance fighter, told that the French administrator Coppolani intends to make
him a herder of the French camel corps as punishment for his defiance; he rides out to meet the
French column instead and sings:

> كبلاني يغيلان مارت عنو ماهو واعي
> داير مني راعي وان ماني كاع الراصي راعي

Kab-lāni y-ghīlān, mārt ʿan-u mā huwā wāʿī / dāyer minnī rāʿī w-an, mā-nī kāʿ er-rāṣī rāʿī
Working EN: "That swaggering Coppolani — his plans are senseless. / He'd make me a herder, when I cannot even herd myself."
Working FR: « Ce Cablani qui se pavane — ses desseins n'ont aucun sens. / Il ferait de moi un berger, moi qui ne sais même pas me garder moi-même. »
Usage: strip quote for the Coppolani chapter (c. 1904–05, Tagant). Sharp, ironic, humanizing.

His second kāf — the single most quotable line of the whole corpus:

> والله عظمت السبحان ماني باغي فالدني يوم
> ماه يومي عند أزوان والل يوم البارود إكوم

Wa-llāh ʿaẓmat is-subḥān, mā-nī bāghī f ed-dunyā yōm / māh yōmī ʿand azwān, wel-lāl yōm el-bārūd ī-kūm
Working EN: "By God, by His greatness — I want no day of this world: / my day is not the day of idle men; my day is the day gunpowder burns."
Working FR: « Par Dieu, par Sa grandeur — je ne veux d'aucun jour de ce monde : / mon jour n'est pas celui des oisifs ; mon jour est celui où brûle la poudre. »
Usage: flagship quote for "The Poets' War" hero banner.

### 1.2 — The Emir of Adrar, Sidi Ahmed ould Ahmeddou (ould Ahmedou ould Aïda), in French prison
Captured after the battle of Tichit (1912), wounded, jailed in Senegal; the jailers serve him food
alone — an unbearable insult to a Moorish emir, for whom eating alone (أكل وحده, "كيف امكروظ")
is the mark of a miser. His prison kāf:

> مارت عندي عني مكبوظ
> نوكل وحدي كيف امكروظ

Mārt ʿandī ʿanī mkabẓ / n-ūkul waḥdī keyf em-karūẓ
Working EN: "Now I am held, bound, / and I eat alone like a miser."
Working FR: « Me voilà retenu, enchaîné, / et je mange seul comme un avare. »
Usage: strip quote — a whole culture's idea of dignity compressed into two lines, from inside a colonial cell.

### 1.3 — Homecoming kāf of the same emir (after release, returning from Senegal)

> حامد لله اللي ابعاد اندر وزين اديار
> وافرح لمحار وعاد زاد يور منب بحجار

Ḥāmed le-llāh el-lī eb-ʿad andar w-azīn ed-diyār / w-efreḥ lem-ḥār w-ʿād zād yōr enb baḥjār
Working EN: "Praise God who has kept Adrar and graced its homes, / gladdened the captive, and brought him back crowned with joy."
Working FR: « Loué soit Dieu qui a gardé l'Adrar et embelli ses demeures, / réjoui le captif et le rend, couronné de joie. »
Usage: pairing with 1.2 — prison then return; the emir answers his own lament.

### 1.4 — Doudou Sék (Ibn el-Megdadad)'s reply / coded warning to the emir
Doudou Sék, the emir's friend (though the French administration's chief interpreter), answers the
homecoming kāf with a verse some read as a warning that Coppolani intended to enter the Adrar:

> حامد لله اللي ابعاد أطار اللمور مور
> كلت بعد ويبور زاد بعد ينزاد اللمور

Ḥāmed le-llāh el-lī eb-ʿad aṭār el-lmūr mūr / kelt baʿd w-ībūr zād baʿd yenzād el-mūr
Working EN: "Praise God who has kept the Tichit road behind — / a road still turns, and turns again, and more road keeps being added."
Working FR: « Loué soit Dieu qui a tenu la piste de Tichit derrière nous — / une piste tourne, tourne encore, et la piste s'allonge sans fin. »
(Original gloss in source: the friend's verses carried a hidden notice that the French ruler planned to push into the Adrar.)
Usage: "Poets' War" — poetry as encrypted intelligence between friends across the colonial frontier.

### 1.5 — Elegy for the fallen emir — Haymouda ould Ngimish
The emir was killed at the battle of Wadiyān al-Kharūb (Wadian Kharoub):

> عكلي لمتن تلاهو كلت عربي يشرح لكلوب
> التالي من لعرب راهو مدفون افوديان الخروب

ʿaklī lemten telāhū, kelt ʿarabī y-sherraḥ le-klūb / et-tālī men leʿrāb rāhū medfūn few-diyān el-kharūb
Working EN: "Understand what I tell you: a man of the Arabs who eased all hearts. / The last of the Arab chiefs now lies buried at Wadian el-Kharoub."
Working FR: « Comprends ce que je te dis : un homme des Arabes qui apaisait tous les cœurs. / Le dernier des chefs arabes repose enterré à Ouadian el-Kharoub. »
Usage: the tebra (lament) voice of the site — grief, not defiance.

### 1.6 — The Oualata demolition anthem — Weld Abduka
Each time the French rebuilt the "khubba" post at Oualata, the townspeople pulled it down, singing
this kāf as an anthem while demolishing:

> لخويب اخلات وانكطع موله
> ويلا بتنات انوله

Le-khwīb ekh-lāt, an-keṭaʿ mūl-u / w-īla betnāt, an-ūl-u
Working EN: "The khubba — down with it, cut off its owner's stake. / And if they raise it again, we'll bring it down again."
Working FR: « La khoubba — à bas, coupe le pieu de son maître. / Et s'ils la relèvent, nous la renverserons encore. »
Usage: popular, non-elite resistance voice; the only "chant of the crowd" in the corpus. Perfect for Oualata chapter.

### 1.7 — Cheikh Saad Bouh (Ma El Aïnin's brother) on the French safe-conduct
Cheikh Saad Bouh bin Sheikh Muhammad Fadil (al-Qalqami) — the famously pro-accommodation brother —
tells how the French ruler of Saint-Louis handed him a signed safe-conduct (تسريح) promising its
bearer would never be wronged, and answers:

> شيخ اندر أعطاني تسريح وكال ان التسريح الصحيح
> يفلش بيه الغايب ويريح من مسل فيه ظلم فيه
> واعرفت أن ذاك الكايل ريح يغير آن كاع أبديه

Shīkh andar aʿṭānī tesrīḥ, w-kāl en-nes-rīḥ eṣ-ṣaḥīḥ / y-felles bīh el-ghāyeb w-erīḥ, men msel fīh ẓlem fīh / w-ʿarraft en-nāk el-kāyel rīḥ, y-ghīr ān kāʿ eb-dīh
Working EN: "The chief of Ndar gave me a safe-conduct and said: this pass is sure — it frees the wanderer and shields him from any wrong. And I knew the speaker lied; my trust stays with God alone."
Working FR: « Le chef de Saint-Louis m'a donné un sauf-conduit et dit : ce laisser-passer est sûr — il libère le voyageur et le couvre de tout tort. Et j'ai su que celui qui parlait mentait : ma confiance reste à Dieu seul. »
Usage: nuance — shows the treaty/accommodation side of the same family; pairs with Ma El Aïnin's defiance.

### 1.8 — Sidiya ould Ahmedou ould Qatrab (ed-Dimani), exile in Timbuktu
Resistance man and major poet, died in exile in Timbuktu (Mali):

> سلكني يالرب من النار عاكب ذ من روغ الكفار
> مركني كلنل اديار ماني باغي عنهم نرحل

Selsknī yā-rab men en-nār, ʿākeb dhā men rūgh el-kuffār / merreknī kollenel ed-diyār, mā-nī bāghī ʿanhom n-raḥel
Working EN: "Deliver me, Lord, from the fire, after those who warred on the unbelievers. / Bind me to every land — I do not want to leave them."
Working FR: « Délivre-moi, Seigneur, du feu, après ceux qui combattirent les infidèles. / Attache-moi à chaque terre — je ne veux pas les quitter. »
Usage: the exile voice — resistance's cost, homesickness in a colonial-imposed banishment.

### 1.9 — Ould Sidi ould Sheikh al-Qadi addresses his war mare Umm Khreis
A resistance fighter singing to his horse, veteran of the battlefields against the colonizer:

> لزمك يم اخريص التجلاج وارفود الوغر ولجلج
> امالك يكون افعلاج يم اخريص امرد الكفار
> وكان اجلجتي ما تحتاج اعليك آن حر ولحرار

L-zemk yem ekhrīṣ et-tejlāj, w-arfūd el-wagh wel-jelj / emālek ykūn ef-ʿlāj, yem ekhrīṣ am-red el-kuffār / w-kān ejljetī mā t-eḥtāj, ʿalīk ān ḥorr wel-ḥorrār
Working EN: "Stay close, O Umm Khreis, through the dust and din, bearing thrust and clash of spears. / Yours be healing, Umm Khreis, you who crush the unbelievers. / And if you linger, you'll not need me — above me you are free, and of the free."
Working FR: « Reste près de moi, ô Oum Khreis, dans la poussière et le tumulte, portant coups et heurts des lances. / À toi la guérison, ô Oum Khreis, toi qui broies les infidèles. / Et si tu t'arrêtes, tu n'auras pas besoin de moi — tu es libre au-dessus de moi, et des libres. »
Usage: the cavalry voice — visceral, unusual for Western audiences, pure Battle-of-Adrar texture.

### 1.10 — Against the French school (year-name "etfarsi")
The year the population was forced to send children to the new French schools; families hid their
sons from "the Christians." A resistance man sings:

> ذ الكافر من متن الشبع تاهم عن كاع ابلا ربع
> قدام حد اللي يسع فيه ألا نشر المعاصي
> غير آن يجعل فيه خير هذ النصراني لمعاصي
> جاحد عن ول يغير ماني جاحد عن راصي

Dhā el-kāfer men metten es-shbaʿ, tāhem ʿan kāʿ belā rebʿ / gdām ḥad el-lī yesʿ fīh, el-lā n-sher el-maʿāṣī / ghīr ān y-jʿel fīh kheyr, hādh en-naṣrānī le-maʿāṣī / jāḥed ʿan wel y-ghīr, mā-nī jāḥed ʿan rāṣī
Working EN: "This unbeliever, once his belly is full, denies all kinship and shares nothing. Before anyone who seeks him, he spreads only sin. May God yet put good in this Christian of sins: he denies his son but is changed — I do not deny my own."
Working FR: « Cet incroyant, le ventre plein, renie tout lien et ne partage rien. Devant quiconque le cherche, il ne sème que le péché. Que Dieu mette pourtant du bien en ce chrétien de péchés : lui renie son fils et change — moi, je ne renie pas les miens. »
Usage: the "war for the children" angle — schools as the occupation's second front (1910s–30s).

### 1.11 — Wajaha (وجاهه), son of Cheikh Ma El Aïnin — mujahid and Hassaniya poet
Per the source, a leading mujahid who saw dialect verse as a weapon to steel fighters; his kāf
"mourning himself" (anticipating his death in battle):

> حامد ربي ماني نكدان بايت متوسد جهادي
> تل الدوكج شرك اجبلان افتيرس من كد أوادي

Ḥāmed rabbī mā-nī negdān, bāyit met-wessed jihādī / tel ed-dūkej sherk ejbelān, ef-tīres men ked awādī
Working EN: "I praise my Lord — I fear no reversal, resting my head upon my jihad. / Thunder cracks the mountains' east, and lightning stabs through the dark of night."
Working FR: « Je loue mon Seigneur — je ne crains aucun revers, la tête posée sur mon djihad. / Le tonnerre fend l'orient des montagnes, et l'éclair poignarde l'obscurité de la nuit. »
**Name caveat:** rimnow gives «وجاهه بن الشيخ ماء العينين»; Ma El Aïnin's documented sons include Ahmed al-Hiba and Murabbih Rabbuh — treat this attribution with care and re-verify before publishing a family tree.

Two praise kāfs by other poets on the same man ("وجاهه يلليلي رايم…", "وجاهه من كوم الرفعه…"),
an elegy by the woman poet Khiwijatna bint Emme («نعرف عن يحفظ ستين…»), and a farewell by Sheikh
ould Ljerb, who died shortly after fighting alongside Muhammad el-Mamoun («يكطع بالدني مدناها…»),
are transcribed in full in `rts_research/pages/rimnow_8970.txt` (items 17–19) — same tag, same source.

### 1.12 — Against the salaried collaborators — anonymous

> لبسو من خمس لبس زين تناعت فالدشر
> يسو غير إديرو خمس بعد اللي ما كلع عشر

Lebsū men khems lebs zīn, tenāʿet f ed-dsher / īsū ghīr īdīrū khems, baʿd el-lī mā kelaʿ ʿeshr
Working EN: "They put on the fine government cloth and strut in the town. / They'd do better to count five — after what ten fingers could not hold back."
Working FR: « Ils ont revêtu le beau drap de l'État et se pavane en ville. / Ils feraient mieux de compter cinq — après ce que dix doigts n'ont pu retenir. »
(Plays on the proverb "إديرو خمس الما كلع عشرة" — what five fingers put in, ten cannot pull out.)
Usage: the civil-resistance voice — shaming auxiliaries, the internal front of the war.

## SECTION 2 — Emirs, the tbal (war drum), and the old order's voice
**[VERIFIED source]** — al-Mokhtar ould Hamid (المختار بن حامد), encyclopedia *Ḥayāt Mūrītānyā*
("Life of Mauritania"), events volume (حوادث السنين), published by the Cheikh Ma El Aïnin heritage
site: https://cheikh-maelainin.com — full PDF local: `rts_research/pdfs/hawadith.pdf` (Arabic).
The encyclopedia builds on Mauritanian chronicles **and French officers' reports** (stated in its intro).

### 2.1 — The tbal: what the drum meant (prose, quotable for UI copy)
> "ويضرب الطبل عند الموريتانيين قديماً لإرشاد الضال والإعلام بالشيء الطارئ … ويعتبر انتزاع الطبل
> في الحروب وأخذه قهراً في عرف القبائل الموريتانية ذات الشوكة من مظاهر الانتصار وإغاظة العدو وإهانته؛
> وهو أبلغ عندهم أحياناً من القتل والتنكيل. ويشكل الطبل رمزاً من رموز السيادة وعنصراً هاماً في نظام
> الإمارة أو القبيلة. وكان لكل إمارة، بل لكل قبيلة طبلها … ويحتفظ الأمير أو شيخ القبيلة بالطبل ويرثه
> عنه من يتولى بعده."
EN: "The drum was beaten of old by the Mauritanians to guide the lost and announce the sudden event… To wrest the drum in war and take it by force was, in the custom of the strong tribes, a sign of victory, a goad and humiliation to the enemy — at times weightier to them than killing and slaughter. The drum is a symbol of sovereignty and a pillar of the emirate's order: every emirate, every tribe, had its drum… The emir keeps it, and the one who succeeds him inherits it."
Also in the same passage: named drum rhythms — أراتيم الفزعة (the alarm rhythm), اخبيط الرحيل (departure), الهدي (recall) — and a recorded quarrel where emir Bakkar ould Amar nearly fought his son-in-law rather than surrender the great drum to his house.
Usage: section intro copy for "the Poets' War" — explains why tbal music (played over the site's battle art) is itself a sovereignty claim, not mere percussion. Also note: tbal double sense = the war-commemoration genre, per M.E.M. Bah's studies of Hassaniya genres.

### 2.2 — Adrar mockery verse, 1909 (last years before the conquest of the Adrar)
**[VERIFIED source]** — *Tārīkh Mūrītānyā al-ḥadīth* (Modern History of Mauritania), p. 276 n.276,
citing al-Mokhtar ould Hamid's encyclopedia p. 141 (local: `rts_research/pdfs/tarikh_modern.pdf`).
When Emir Muhammad al-Habib's counter-raid against the "Ghazwat al-Malhs" (the emir of Adrar's
"Monkasa" total-sack raid of 1909) was slow to come, the people of Adrar mocked:

> ما بان ان اله يمتان
> محمد الحبيب السلطان
> ما ج فيه ألا ج فالصيف
> شتين فالخط أفغان

Mā bān en el-hayy yemtān / Muḥammad el-Ḥabīb is-sulṭān / mā jī fīh el-lā jī f eṣ-ṣīf / shtīn fel-khaṭṭ efgān
Working EN: "It seems the sleeping man still sleeps — Muhammad al-Habib, the sultan: he comes only with the summer's coming; winter in the Khatṭ, he does not appear."
Working FR: « On dirait que le dormeur dort encore — Muhammad al-Habib, le sultan : il ne vient qu'avec l'été ; l'hiver venu dans le Khatt, point d'apparition. »
Usage: shows the war-poetry tradition alive and political in 1909 — and that emirs themselves were its targets, not just the French. (Inter-emirate rivalry, not anti-colonial — present it as such.)

## SECTION 3 — Cheikh Ma El Aïnin and his house (classical Arabic strand)
### 3.1 — Ma El Aïnin as poet — VERIFIED person, VERIFIED verse output
- **[VERIFIED source]** The same encyclopedia (hawadith.pdf, biography section) describes him:
  "لم يغادر الشيخ ماء العينين حضرة والده بالحوض إلا وهو عالم لا يشق غباره… وشاعر مفلق"
  — "he left his father's zāwiya in the Ḥawḍ already an unmatched scholar… **and a brilliant poet**",
  and dates his 1274 AH departure and the later building of **Samara (Smara)** and his organizing of the resistance.
- His surviving corpus is theological/family verse, e.g. "The Sixty-Six Beliefs" (*العقائد الستة والستون*),
  studied in: Dr. Hamza Muallawi (د. حمزة معلاوي), "قراءة في نظم الشيخ ماء العينين (تـ. 1328هـ) العقائد الستة والستون",
  Al-Idāh journal (Sheikh Zayed Center, Peshawar University), vol. 2, no. 42, 19 Dec 2024 —
  summary at https://cheikh-maelainin.com/?p=9062 ; see also the Ma El Aïnin colloquium https://maelainincolloquium.com/
- A short rajaz couplet of his ("لما تناثر عليّ الهمُّ") is indexed at https://mizanalarab.com/poem/143389
**Honest status:** we verified he wrote verse and that his poems exist in manuscripts/print — but no
verified *anti-French* Arabic verse by him was retrievable online this session. Do NOT quote him in
battle-defiance mode without the actual line in hand.
### 3.2 — His son Ahmed al-Hiba and the family war effort
Battle timeline (6 Sept 1912, Sidi Bou Othman defeat) documented in secondary press; no verified verse
by al-Hiba found online. Wajaha item 1.11 is the only Ma El Aïnin-family *verse* in hand — single-source, name needs re-verification.

## SECTION 5 — Modern canonization (use only inside a dated "later echo" panel) — [ANACHRONISM RISK]
All below are authentic Sahrawi resistance-culture artifacts but post-date 1934 — display with explicit date labels, never as 1902–34 voices:
- **Bashir Ali** (b. 1947) — "the Poet of the Revolution," Polisario-era; recordings made Nov 2022–Feb 2023 held via the Northumbria University Western Sahara Poetry project: https://hosting.northumbria.ac.uk/westernsaharapoetry/featured-poets (recordings archived with the British Library).
- **Zaim Allal** (b. 1957), **Khadijattu** (b. 1973) — same project; women's and exile generations.
- **Ali Abd-el-Rahman** — poems on the **Zemla massacre of 17 June 1970** (pre-Polisario nationalist turning point, Spanish era).
- **Al Hadra** (Al-Baath? — "La Aurora"), 1970s Sahrawi cultural-magazine poetry movement — documented via sandblast-arts.org / Peace News profiles.
- Hassaniya referendum kāfs (1958 "yes/no" de Gaulle referendum — rimnow items 9–11, incl. Ahmed Salem ould Bebbout el-Ghilani and el-Mokhtar ould Hdarar: "Vote 'no' to colonization…") — anti-colonial continuum, 24+ years after our period.
- Independence-era canonization (rimnow items 22–23, el-Mokhtar ould Ibrahim Khalil el-Abyeri, 28 Nov 1960 independence poem) — for the epilogue panel only.
