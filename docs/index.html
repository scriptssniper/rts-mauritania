Concept Stage

THE UNDEFEATED SOIL

1480–1934 · Bilad Shinqit · from Tondibi to the last Reguibat rezzou

A scattered nation of camel nomads, scholar-saints and tribal federations fought one of history's largest empires for thirty-two years — no artillery, no railways, no cities — and was never defeated on its own soil. France took the wells. It never took the allegiance.

The Pitch

In 1902 France began absorbing a desert country the size of France and Spain combined — and meant to do it without battles. An Arabist conqueror took three emirates with treaties and sermons; a single five-minute ambush killed him in a palmery at Tidjikja, and the conquest froze for three years. What followed was not a war of fronts but a war of water: thirty-two years in which the world's finest army could hold every fort it built and still die of thirst between them.

THE UNDEFEATED SOIL is a point-control RTS with no worker economy and no base building. The map is a lattice of wells, oases, pastures and caravan routes. Every unit carries water; away from a friendly well it bleeds a thirst meter until it dies. At Nouarmach in July 1908, thirteen tirailleurs fell in combat and eleven more died of thirst afterward — that statistic is a game mechanic, not flavor text.

The asymmetry is the design. The resistance plays mobility: lightning rezzou raids, tribal allegiances won and held, caravans raided and herds driven, gun-runs down from Morocco. France plays infrastructure: a web of mud-brick borj forts extending its supply radius post by post, columns budgeted from Saint-Louis, subsidies and propaganda splitting tribe from tribe. Same territory, opposite grammars of war.

A pre-colonial prologue teaches the world before the flag arrives — the emirates' own wars, the gum and salt trade, the rules of the raid — so that when France appears, it appears as what it was to the people of Bilad Shinqit: a disruption arriving in a world already at war with itself.

The Side That Would Not Be Ruled

The Resistance

The House of Ma El Aïnin

A saint's dynasty against an empire
  • Ma El Aïnin (1831–1910) — scholar of a hundred and forty books, builder of the unfinished holy city of Smara, proclaimer of the jihad. A living banner: his baraka made units unbreakable, his death began the collapse.
  • Sidi Seghir (d. 1905) — the dream-vow assassin. Twenty-seven warriors, five minutes after night prayer, and the conquest froze for three years.
  • Ahmed al-Hiba, "the Blue Sultan" — the son who took Marrakech in 1912 with ten thousand men, and lost two thousand of them in an afternoon.
  • Agdaf — vengeance of the burned city: when Smara fell in 1913, his reprisals killed forty men across three posts.
  • Merebbi Rebbu — keeper of the remnant after 1919, patron of the last great raids.
  • Emir Sidi Ahmed Ould Ahmed Ayda — the tragedy arc: installed, captured, restored as a puppet, defected, dead in the desert. His allegiance swings the whole Adrar.

Tribes & Tactics

How the desert fought
  • The Rguibat — the irreconcilables. Sacked Tindouf in 1896, struck Port-Étienne twice in the 1920s, deserted wholesale in 1932, and rode the final 6,000-km grand rezzou of 1934.
  • Ouled Delim, Tekna, Tenaki — the raiding nations of the north; the méharistes who walked off with their weapons rather than fight their kin.
  • The rezzou — the lightning camel raid: converge, strike, take herds and captives, vanish before pursuit can mount.
  • Well-denial — the desert fights over water, not terrain. Hold or spoil the wells and columns die slowly, on their own.
  • The azalaï economy — salt caravans crossing the erg: resource convoys on legs, for escort or ambush.
  • The Moroccan gun-run — the sultan's steamship Turki landing rifles at Tarfaya; in 1916, a German submarine's guns. External supply as a campaign lane.

Ahmed al-Hiba — The Blue Sultan

c. 1876 – 23 June 1919 · eldest son of Ma El Aïnin

The resistance's most dramatic arc. From the ruins of his father's cause he raised an army of the far north — Tekna, Ouled Delim, Rguibat — crossed into Morocco, and on 14 August 1912 entered Marrakech with ten thousand men, where he was proclaimed Sultan.

It lasted three weeks. At Sidi Bou Othmane, on 6 September 1912, Mangin's spahis and tirailleurs destroyed the army — some two thousand warriors dead — and the Blue Sultan's reign ended before it had begun a second month. The jihad retreated into the desert for good.

He fought on from the Anti-Atlas for seven more years, refusing both French pardons and Spanish pensions, until his death at Kerdous on 23 June 1919. In game terms: the fastest levy-multiplier leader in the cast — mass armies raised quickly, quality paid for in blood.

Voices of the War

The Other Front

The Poets' War

This war was fought in verse as much as in gunpowder. Hassaniya oral poetry — the kāf — was the era's newspaper: defiance, coded intelligence, mockery and grief, carried by memory from camp to camp. What survives is fragmentary; the great print collections (Norris 1968, 1986) remain unmined — the v2 corpus of this project.

Honest gaps: laments for the fall of Smara and the verse of the Char Bouba war survive, if at all, only in print archives — one more reason this history needs more games, not fewer.

The Counterpoint — and the Other Half of the Game

The Colonizer's Method

Penetrate by Speech, Not by Rifle

Coppolani's political conquest
  • Xavier Coppolani — the Corsican Arabist who took Trarza, Brakna and Tagant with treaties, subsidies and sermons before a rezzou ended him in 1905.
  • Divide and rule — over eight hundred recognized chiefs and sub-chiefs, tribute switched to rebates, a captured emir restored to a hollowed throne.
  • Es-Saada — the Tangier newspaper run as a character-assassination campaign against Ma El Aïnin. Propaganda as a playable lane.

The Column & the Lattice

Gouraud's answer
  • Col. Henri Gouraud — the man who replaced persuasion with the camel column and the counter-rezzou: the raid's tactic under a flag.
  • The borj lattice — Nouakchott, Tidjikja, Moudjeria, Inchiri, Atar: a web of mud-brick forts extending supply and safe water, post by post.
  • Water discipline — the column as a moving fortress with its own thirst; every march a wager against the wells.
  • Thirst as a weapon — and the desert loaned it to whoever held the water. The asymmetry is the game's design.
Order of Battle

Arms & Men

The Resistance

trade guns, taken guns, no guns to spare
  • Remington Rolling Block — the trade rifle of the Sahara, .43 Spanish, bought up the Morocco caravan lane. Verified
  • Captured and surplus Gras — battlefield pick-ups (Niémilane, Damané, Liboirat) and post-1886 French surplus sold north from Saint-Louis: same 11mm cartridge as the enemy's own second line. Plausible
  • Moukhala flintlocks — the muzzle-loader still rides with poorer warriors and oasis levies. Plausible
  • Lances, takouba, nimcha — the camel lance is the rezzou's battle arm; the takouba broadsword is rank made visible — nobles carry swords, tributaries carry spears. Verified
  • No artillery. Ever. No siege train: Moors besiege borjs but almost never storm them. Verified
  • Ammunition is the true bottleneck — cartridges ride the same caravans as the guns; warbands fire slow and conserve. Verified

France

a poor colonial ministry's arsenal
  • Gras Mle 1874 — 11mm single-shot, black powder; colonial troops drew surplus Gras long after the metropolitan army moved on. Carried until 1907. Verified
  • Berthier Mle 1907 — adopted for the tirailleurs sénégalais in 1907: 3-shot clip, smokeless 8mm. The tech-tree event of the whole campaign — after 1907, parity ends. Verified
  • Modèle 1892 revolver — the officer's sidearm. Plausible
  • 2×65mm Mle 1906 mountain guns + a 2-gun machine-gun section — pack-transportable; the column's treasure, and the true tech gap. Plausible
  • Bayonets and sabers — épée-baïonnettes; light-cavalry sabers for the spahis. Plausible
  • Never the Lebel, never the Legion — colonial units drew from the Colonies ministry, not the War Ministry. Verified

The design insight: before 1907 this is a small-arms parity war — both sides single-shot, black powder, ~200 m practical range. The real asymmetry was never the rifle. It was logistics, water, and the 700 camels behind every French column.

Tirailleur sénégalais

Dark-blue tunic and trousers, red sash, red chéchia; khaki drill from 1898. Famously barefoot. The backbone of every column.

Méhariste

Khaki or white drill, baggy trousers, chéchia or covered képi — and the cheich scarf: French camel troops veiling like the blue men they pursue.

Spahis sénégalais

The red burnous over khaki — Toucouleur recruits from the old Garde Rouge; Fourn's squadron rode the 1908 campaigns.

French officer

Colonial whites or khaki, cork casque colonial, cheich in the desert. A tiny cadre, deliberately forward — and deliberately sniped.

Hassane warrior

Indigo tagelmust wound over mouth and brow, indigo bubu over short trousers. The dye bleeds into skin: the "blue men." No armor — distance is armor.

The Algerian cavalry, honestly framed: a 2e escadron de spahis algériens rode in the colonnes volantes of 1908–09 Verified — and the Chaamba méhariste companies of the Algerian Sahara (1902) were the model for camel warfare, not men seconded to Mauritania. Mauritania's camel companies were home-grown: Gouraud mounted Sudanese tirailleur sections in 1902–03. The post-WWII white-képi méhariste iconography does not belong to this war.

Rezzou doctrine Verified

Converging camel columns, dawn strike, take herds and captives — not positions — then disperse into the vacuum. Template: Gandon 1857. Terminal form: the 6,000-km Reguibat grand rezzou of 1934.

Counter-rezzou Verified

Gouraud's answer: the raid's own tactic under a flag. Camel columns as desert police — seize the wells, threaten the herds, negotiate tribe by tribe.

Column anatomy Verified

700+ camels of logistics, watering order, well-to-well marches, ~30–40 km a day. Nouarmach, 24 July 1908: a fight won — then eleven tirailleurs died of thirst.

Borj defense Verified

Square stone posts and zariba night-laagers. Small garrisons hold: a corporal and seven men stopped the Gandon rezzou. Besieged often, stormed almost never.

Officer sniping Verified

The European cadre is tiny and deliberately forward — every loss is real. Niémilane 1907, Damané 1908, Liboirat 1913: sixty dead in a morning.

Parley culture Verified

Sieges end in negotiation more often than assault, and tribes flip for tribute. The "forty horses" norm is an 18th-century precolonial precedent — it works on tribes, never on columns.

Gameplay Pillars

Wells Are Resources

Capture points are wells, oases, palmaries and caravan stops. Deny a well to an enemy column and you kill it slowly — no assault required.

Herds Are Economy

Camels and cattle graze controlled pasture. Wealth walks — it can be raided, driven off, and taken back. The azalaï salt caravans are resource convoys on legs.

Allegiance Is Recruitment

No barracks, no unit spam. Each tribe that swears to you is a manpower pool with its own flavor — Rguibat raiders fight nothing like Kounta levies. Lose the tribe's allegiance, lose the army.

The Borj Lattice

France builds no cities either. A web of small desert forts extends supply radius and safe water, post by post — Nouakchott, Tidjikja, Moudjeria, Atar. Cut the web and the desert returns.

The Rezzou

The lightning raid: converge, strike, take herds and captives, and be gone before pursuit can mount. France's answer was the contre-rezzou — the same tactic with a flag.

Thirst

Every unit carries water. Distance from a friendly well is a decay timer. Heat is the third army in every battle, and it never negotiates.

The Campaign

Act 0 · The Old Caravan World

Oualata and Tondibi

1480 – 1591

The prologue opens in 1480: the Mossi besiege and sack Oualata — and the townsmen regroup, pursue the raiders into the bush and take their captives back. A ready-made rescue tutorial. Then Tondibi, 13 March 1591: Judar Pasha's arquebusiers and cannon destroy the Songhai empire, seven centuries of Sudanese power end in an afternoon, and the caravan cities of Bilad Shinqit begin their long, slow decay.

Act 0 · The Thirty Years' War of the Desert

Char Bouba

1644 – 1674

Nasr ad-Din's Sanhadja confederation against the Banu Hassan: victories at Intuji and Jiwa, the imam's death at Tirtillass, four doomed successors falling one by one — and the Treaty of Tin Yedfad, by which the defeated "permanently renounce arms." Out of the war come the emirates of Trarza and Brakna, the hassane and znaga social order, and the tribute rules France will one day exploit.

Act 0 · Prologue

Before the Flag

1638 – 1901

The world before invasion: emirates and marabouts, the gum trade down the Senegal river and the coutume tribute born of it, salt caravans across the erg. The tutorial era — master the rezzou, the wells and the tribute, because every one of those tools is about to be aimed at an empire.

Act I

The Charmeur

1902 – 12 May 1905

Xavier Coppolani conquers Trarza, Brakna and Tagant with treaties, subsidies and sermons — "penetrate by speech, not by rifle." It ends in a palmery at Tidjikja: a dream-vow assassin, twenty-seven warriors, five minutes after night prayer. The conquest freezes for three years.

Act II

The Column

1905 – 1909

The harassment war: rezzous bleed the occupier column by column — Niémilane, Damané, Nouarmach and its eleven dead of thirst. Colonel Gouraud answers with camel columns and the counter-rezzou, forces the Hamdoun pass, and takes Atar on 9 January 1909.

Act III

The Long Decay

1910 – 1934

Ma El Aïnin's field army dies at Tadla in Morocco and the saint himself follows — but the desert is not done. Smara burns in 1913 and vengeance answers at Liboirat, Tagliat and Bou-Telis. A puppet emir defects, a méhariste company deserts, and the last great Rguibat rezzou rides 6,000 kilometers before the "pacification" closes at Tindouf, 31 May 1934.

The Map of the War

The Map Is the Enemy

The Land

Every tile below is a terrain type, a movement rule, and a tactic at once. The Hassaniya names are the game's map vocabulary.

عِرْق 'irq · Erg

Sand sea: high dunes, no surface water, thorn scrub only on the flanks.

Camel-only. Columns skirt the margins; rezzous vanish into them. Nouarmach-class thirst gaps open where ergs part the wells.

Reg

Firm desert pavement of gravel, scattered acacia.

Fast for everyone — the meeting-engagement tile. Both sides see far; there is no cover.

Hamada

Bare stone plateau, waterless, heat-radiating.

Dead ground — the Adrar's tabletop. Nothing lives above the escarpment edge without wells.

وادي wādī · Oued

Dry valley: lines of acacia and palm, water holes after rain, shallow wells.

The ambush corridor. Vegetation is cover — the palmery assassination terrain of Coppolani's last walk, Tidjikja 1905.

سبخة sebkha · Salt flat

Salt-clay pan, sometimes seasonal water — all of it unpotable, boggy after rain.

The water-trap tile: it looks like water and kills the thirsty. Impassable when wet.

Escarpments & passes

Cliff belts with rare ramps: Amogjar, Djouk, Hamdoun.

Chokepoints. Whoever holds the pass owns the plateau — Hamdoun, 8 January 1909.

Palm oasis

Gardens, date palms, permanent wells, a stone borj — Tidjikja, Atar, Chinguetti, Oujeft.

Capture objectives: wells, ambush cover in the dense palmery, manuscripts and zaouias.

Chemama · river belt

Flood-recession farms and river towns along the Senegal — Bakel, Podor, Richard Toll. Tsetse belt.

The French base-line: river steamers are their logistics spine — and camels sicken here. A biome-gated army.

Sahel transition

Thorn savanna; grass after the hivernage rains, permanent wells on the Kiffa–Aioun axis.

Transhumance tile: herds ascend north with the rains — and a concentration of herds is a rezzou magnet.

Aoukar basin

Dead-lake depression between the Tagant cliffs and the Dhar Néma; rich cold-season pasture, seasonal ponds.

The raid economy's heartland — the basin's herds feed both sides' war logistics.

About the Project

THE UNDEFEATED SOIL is a concept-stage game design. Target engine: Godot 4 (open source, no royalties). Single-player campaign first; no release date exists. Design docs, the historical research backbone and the character bible live in this repository.

Historical basis

The campaign follows the documented record of the French pacification of Mauritania. Where sources disagree — siege dates, the year an emir was captured — the disagreements are flagged in the research document rather than smoothed over. One caution from the research is baked into the design: the war's true asymmetry was logistics and water, not weapons — both sides carried comparable rifles.
