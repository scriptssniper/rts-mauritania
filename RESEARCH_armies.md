## 1. FRENCH WEAPONS

### 1.1 Rifles — the two-era story (biggest correction)

| Weapon | Era in Mauritania | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Fusil Gras Mle 1874** (11×59mmR, single-shot, black powder) | c.1902 → ~1907 | **[VERIFIED — fr.wiki Fusil Gras + Berthier articles]** Regulation 7 Jul 1874; ~2.5M made; after Lebel adoption (1886) relegated to second-line and colonial use. Colonial troops were equipped by the **Ministère des Colonies** (poor budget), which "récupère un grand nombre de fusils et carabines Mle 1874 dits Gras" — tirailleurs sénégalais carried Gras long after the metropolitan army had Lebels. |
| **Fusil Berthier Mle 1907 "colonial" / "de tirailleur sénégalais"** | from **1907** (→1930s) | **[VERIFIED — fr.wiki Fusils et mousquetons Berthier]** "En 1907, le ministère des colonies décide d'adopter un fusil Berthier… pour les tirailleurs sénégalais." Lebel-length, 3-shot en-bloc Mannlicher clip, fires the **8mm Lebel smokeless cartridge**. This is the actual rifle of the Adrar campaign (Dec 1908–1909) tirailleurs. |
| **Fusil Lebel Mle 1886/93** | **NOT carried** | **[VERIFIED — institutional]** Colonial troops did not draw from the War Ministry; the wiki states it explicitly for the Indochinese tirailleurs (same Colonies ministry) and the 1907 Berthier was created precisely because tirailleurs sénégalais had no Lebel-length modern rifle. No Lebel in a Mauritania column pre-1914. |

Gras ballistics: 25g lead ball, 450 m/s, practical range ~200m, standard dotation 78 rounds [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Fusil Gras]. Berthier Mle 1907: 3-shot clip, ~640 m/s, smokeless — a genuine firepower inflection point **in-period (1907)**. Game-relevant: pre-1907 French vs Remington-armed Moors is near-parity (both single-shot black-powder); post-1907 the French gain sustained volley fire.

### 1.2 Bayonets
- **Épée-baïonnette Mle 1874** (crossguard, ~44cm blade) for the Gras; older yatagan Mle 1866 sabre-baïonnette in residual use [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Fusil Gras].
- Lebel-pattern **épée-baïonnette Mle 1886/93** for the Berthier Mle 1907 colonial [PLAUSIBLE-inv — standard pairing of the Berthier 1902/1907 colonial family].

### 1.3 Revolvers (officers)
- **Revolver d'ordonnance Mle 1892** 8mm — standard French officer/NCO sidearm from 1892 [PLAUSIBLE-inv; Mauritania-specific carry not documented].
- Older **Chamelot-Delvigny Mle 1873/74** 11mm still widespread in colonial stocks [PLAUSIBLE-inv].

### 1.4 Machine guns (correction to "negligible")
- The **Colonne de l'Adrar, constituted 5 Dec 1908 at Moudjeria, included "le génie, l'artillerie, une section de mitrailleuses et une ambulance"** — Gouraud's own account, *La Pacification de la Mauritanie* (1910) [VERIFIED — Gallica full-text search snippet, ark bpt6k57844898; OCR text itself Cloudflare-blocked, so the exact passage page is unretrievable — treat composition as verified, details below as inferred].
- Type: by Dec 1908 the French army's gun for mobile/colonial sections was the **St-Étienne Mle 1907 T** or the fragile **Puteaux Mle 1905**; the Hotchkiss Portatif (Benét-Mercié, Mle 1909) entered colonial service from 1909 [PLAUSIBLE-inv]. Which gun marched to the Adrar: [UNCERTAIN].
- Scale: a French MG "section" of the era = **2 guns** [PLAUSIBLE-inv].

### 1.5 Artillery (correction to "negligible")
- Artillery element confirmed present in the Adrar column [VERIFIED — Gouraud, as above]. The earlier "negligible — a couple light pieces" note in `RESEARCH_campaign_history.md` should be revised to: **present but light**.
- Most probable type: **canon de montagne 65mm Mle 1906 (BMP)** — adopted 1906 specifically for mountain/colonial columns, pack-transportable by mule/camel [PLAUSIBLE-inv; Mauritania-specific type [UNCERTAIN]]. Alternative: De Bange 80mm Mle 1878 mountain gun, older stock [PLAUSIBLE-inv].
- Number per column: likely 2 pieces [PLAUSIBLE-inv; exact count [UNCERTAIN — in Gouraud 1910/d'Andurain, texts not retrieved]].

### 1.6 Sabers / edged (cavalry)
- Spahis: light-cavalry sabers of the Mle 1822/96 family, and officers' oriental sabers [PLAUSIBLE-inv].
- Tirailleur NCOs sometimes carried the sabre-baïonquette pattern in parade; campaign use of épée-baïonnette only [PLAUSIBLE-inv].

### 2.2 Traditional arms
- **Takouba** — the Saharan broadsword: ~1m straight double-edged blade, central/western Sahara and Sahel (Tuareg, Hassane Arabs, Hausa, Fulani); historically a noble-status weapon, diffusing downward by the 19th c. [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Takouba]. The prestige sidearm of the hassane class.
- **Nimcha** — Moroccan/NW-African sabre with curved blade and distinctive hilt [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Nimcha; Buttin 1939]. Present via Morocco trade.
- **Lance (camel lancer)** — the main battle arm of the precolonial rezzou: Gandon 1857 raid = 400–500 men, ~50 horses, i.e. a camel-lancer core [VERIFIED — Raid de Gandon, fr.wiki; see `RESEARCH_precolonial.md`].
- **Daggers**, fighting sticks — ubiquitous [PLAUSIBLE-inv].
- **Shields** (leather/otter-hide round shields, Tuareg/Arab pattern) — largely **obsolete by the 1900s** against breechloaders; retained ceremonially or by poorer levies [PLAUSIBLE — Buttin-era arms literature; continued battlefield use [UNCERTAIN]].
- **No artillery, no siege train** — Moors besiege posts but rarely take them (already in campaign doc) [VERIFIED-pattern].

### 2.3 Ammunition & arms supply chains (the scandal lane)
1. **Morocco / the Sultan**: Ma El Aïnin "armé et financé par le sultan du Maroc Moulay Abdelaziz" (from 1898); later supplied via Abdelhafid; the *Turki* steamer ran guns to Tarfaya; Smara (from 1898) built with an arms store [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Ma El Aïnin + campaign doc]. Cartridge resupply rode the same caravans.
2. **Saint-Louis commercial houses**: the "compagnie commerciale" traffic in arms (and slaves) to the Moors — the scandal tied to Coppolani's assassination context (12 May 1905, Tidjikja palmery) [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Coppolani/Désiré-Vuillemin; LOC study]. Internal French scandal — a game mechanic already noted in campaign doc.
3. **Cap Juby / Spanish zone**: Ma El Aïnin seized Donald MacKenzie's trading post there in 1898 — stock capture [VERIFIED event; arms inference PLAUSIBLE].
4. **French surplus Gras** entering the same market after 1886 [PLAUSIBLE — §2.1].
Ammunition drought is the resistance's true bottleneck: they fire slow, conserve, and melt powder horns — game: ammo-as-timer for warbands.

## 3. FRENCH UNIFORMS & ORDER OF BATTLE

### 3.1 Tirailleurs sénégalais (the backbone)
[VERIFIED — Delpérier, « Les tirailleurs sénégalais 1857–1914 », *Armées de l'Histoire–Uniformes* #83, 1984, cited in en.wiki]
- 1857–1889: dark-blue zouave-style jacket with yellow braiding.
- **From 1889 (i.e., our whole era): loose dark-blue tunic + dark-blue trousers, red sash (ceinture), chéchia (red fez).** White trousers substituted in hot season.
- **Light khaki drill field dress adopted 1898** — so both exist 1902–1914; the iconography of the Mauritania columns is mixed khaki/blue with the chéchia retained; drab fez cover for field service [VERIFIED — en.wiki, Jouineau 2009].
- **Fez color debate**: the tirailleur headgear is properly **chéchia** (red fez). Tassel color: tirailleur units wore the blue tassel on red chéchia [UNCERTAIN — iconographic convention; per-unit plates (Jouineau) not retrieved. Safest game reading: red fez, dark-blue tassel].
- **Bare feet**: tirailleurs sénégalais famously campaigned and fought barefoot (well-attested for WWI; period photos for AOF) [PLAUSIBLE-strong]. Sandals (nées) issued to méharistes later.

### 3.2 Méharistes / groupes nomades (Mauritania version)
- **Era-corrected**: the famous white-képi/blue-gandoura méhariste kit belongs to the **post-WWII Legion Saharan companies — do NOT use for 1902–1914** [VERIFIED — the fr.wiki Compagnies méharistes sahariennes uniform section describes the post-1945 era].
- Origin in Mauritania: **Gouraud transformed Sudanese tirailleur sections into camel-mounted companies** (from his 1902–03 reorganization), before/independent of the Algerian-Saharan model [VERIFIED — Hulot 1908, cited in campaign-history research].
- Dress in our era [PLAUSIBLE-inv, era-plausible composition]: khaki or white drill jacket, baggy trousers, **chéchia** or colonial képi with white cover, **cheich** (tagelmust scarf) adopted on column — the irony: French méharistes veiling like the "blue men" they pursue. Barefoot/nail-sandal riding noted in the existing campaign doc [PLAUSIBLE — carried from earlier research; iconography].

### 3.3 Spahis sénégalais
- **2e escadron de spahis sénégalais created 15 Aug 1902** (Ségou → Saint-Louis); recruited largely among Toucouleurs; lineage from the "Garde Rouge" — **red burnous** as the distinctive garment [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Spahis sénégalais; 1889 campaign-dress engraving]. Capt. Gaston Fourn's squadron campaigned in Mauritania 1908 [VERIFIED — campaign doc/fr.wiki Gaston Fourn].
- Dress: red burnous over khaki/white drill, chéchia [VERIFIED-burnous; composition PLAUSIBLE].

### 3.4 Officers / Affaires indigènes
- European officers in white drill or khaki, **casque colonial** (cork sun helmet) on column, képi at post; cheich in the desert [PLAUSIBLE-inv]. Gouraud's officer cadre was tiny and deliberately exposed to sniping (Oglat-Saiet 1908, Niémilane 1907 — campaign doc) [VERIFIED].

### 3.5 ORDER OF BATTLE — and the ALGERIAN CAVALRY question

(a) **Spahis algériens in Mauritania: YES, at squadron (escadron) strength.**
- A **2e escadron de spahis algériens** is cited among the camel-mounted troops of Gouraud's colonnes volantes of the Adrar campaign 1908–09 [VERIFIED-secondary — search synthesis over Gouraud's *Mauritanie-Adrar* (Plon, 1945) and its digital library record; primary page not retrieved → keep unit designation at PLAUSIBLE-strength].
- **1er régiment de spahis algériens' drapeau carries NO Mauritania inscription** (honors: Maroc 1907–1913 etc.) — consistent with detached squadrons not earning a regimental honor [VERIFIED-negative — fr.wiki 1er spahis algériens].
- One earlier lead (3e régiment escadron detached to Mauritania c.1908, France Archives finding aid) could not be confirmed — page JS/cookie-walled [UNCERTAIN].

(b) **Tirailleurs algériens: NO evidence of presence in Mauritania 1902–1914.** AOF's native infantry was the tirailleurs sénégalais; Algerian tirailleurs served in Morocco, not Mauritania [VERIFIED-negative — absence across all retrieved OOB sources].

(c) **Chaamba/Touat méhariste origin — the model, not the men (yet).**
- **Law of 30 March 1902**: creation of the Algerian-Saharan méhariste companies (5 companies, ~68 méharis each, Affaires indigènes officers), Chaamba recruits; Laperrine's 1 Apr 1905 decree made them fully mounted [VERIFIED — fr.wiki Compagnies méharistes sahariennes + Hulot 1908].
- Mauritania's camel units derive from **Gouraud's transformation of Sudanese tirailleur sections (1902–03)**, i.e. a parallel, home-grown creation [VERIFIED — Hulot 1908 via campaign research].
- **Were Algerian Chaamba riders seconded into Mauritanian companies?** The practice existed in the Algerian Sahara (Chaamba cameleers/guides as the model), but no retrieved source documents Chaamba individuals riding in Mauritanian companies [UNCERTAIN — leads: ANOM FRANOM 12136, Gouraud 1945 memoir; plausible for a handful of guides/méharis, not formed contingents].

(d) **Irregular auxiliaries**: locally recruited partisans/tribal contingents used by Gouraud as intelligence screens and negotiated escort [PLAUSIBLE — pattern of the "politique des tribus"; formal mokhazni (Moroccan-style native auxiliaries) is a Moroccan-Protectorate term, not documented for Mauritania [UNCERTAIN]].

(e) **Uniform distinction, if you render both spahi types**: spahis algériens = metropolitan-army dress (short dark-blue braided jacket, red baggy sarouel, chéchia, white or red burnous) vs spahis sénégalais = colonial, **red burnous over khaki/white** [mixed: burnous VERIFIED; algériens jacket PLAUSIBLE-inv].

French ground truth for the game: **tirailleurs sénégalais (Gras→Berthier 1907) + méhariste companies + spahis sénégalais + detached spahis algériens squadron(s) + tiny European officer cadre + 2 mountain guns + 2 MGs + génie + ambulance.**

## 4. RESISTANCE DRESS

- **Hassane warrior kit**: indigo **tagelmust** (veil) wound as turban+mouth-veil, indigo **bubu/derra** (loose robe) over short trousers, leather sandals or barefoot; the indigo dyes skin — the "**blue men**" (Randau's Coppolani biography is literally titled *Un Corse d'Algérie chez les hommes bleus*) [VERIFIED-idiom; garment set VERIFIED-general Saharan ethnography].
- Indigo tagelmust = both status and practical desert camouflage; white or undyed robes for poorer tribes and camp followers [PLAUSIBLE].
- **Armor: none.** Mail/lamellar is pre-19th-century memory; 1900s warriors rely on distance, speed, veils [PLAUSIBLE].
- **Heraldic shields: obsolete by the 1900s** (see §2.2) — keep only as trophy/regalia props [PLAUSIBLE].
- **Tribal banners**: standards existed in Saharan warfare (emir's standard, marabout's banner) [PLAUSIBLE — attested in Saharan warfare generally; specific Idaw Ali/Reguibat vexillology [UNCERTAIN]]. Ma El Aïnin's jihad had a genuine banner-and-drum charisma apparatus [PLAUSIBLE — Sufi mobilization pattern; tbal already VERIFIED in poetry doc].
- **Takouba** as the visible class marker: nobles (hassane) sword-armed, znaga tributaries with spears/lances, haratin and slaves as camp labor [VERIFIED-structure — precolonial doc; takouba nobility VERIFIED].
- Women: camp logistics, water, loot of the rezzou — captives the real prize (Gandon 1857: ~80 women/children) [VERIFIED].

## 5. TACTICS — BOTH SIDES

### 5.1 Rezzou doctrine (resistance)
- Template = **Raid de Gandon 15–16 May 1857**: 400–500 riders, ~50 horses (camel-lancer core), ~10 killed, ~80 captives, burned French factory, gone before pursuit; blocked at Nder only by a corporal + 7 men in a blockhaus [VERIFIED — campaign doc/fr.wiki Raid de Gandon].
- Structure: converging fast camel columns, strike at dawn, target herds/captives not positions, disperse into the vacuum [VERIFIED-pattern].
- Terminal form: the 1934 Reguibat "grand rezzou" — 6,000 km, 800 cattle, 270 camels, 10 slaves [VERIFIED — campaign doc].

### 5.2 Counter-rezzou / police-column war (French)
- Gouraud 1907→: camel-mounted tirailleur columns + méhariste companies as "police of the desert"; aim quoted: "not permanent occupation… a police column to purge the country of its troublemakers" [VERIFIED — campaign doc].
- Method: seize/anchor wells, threaten herds (the tribes' capital), negotiate tribe-by-tribute (Coppolani's original "politique des tribus" continued by force).

### 5.3 Column anatomy — the Adrar column (5 Dec 1908, Moudjeria)
- **700+ camels of logistics**; tirailleurs sénégalais mounted à dos de chameau + méhariste companies; **génie, artillerie (2 light pieces, PLAUSIBLE), section de mitrailleuses (2 guns, PLAUSIBLE), ambulance** [composition VERIFIED — Gouraud 1910 snippet; camel count VERIFIED — fr.wiki/campaign doc; gun counts PLAUSIBLE].
- Water discipline = the column's heartbeat: well-to-well marches, watering order, camel water trains. **Nouarmach 24 Jul 1908: 13 killed in combat, then 11 more tirailleurs die of thirst** — the thirst mechanic [VERIFIED — campaign doc].
- Daily rate ~30–40 km for a mixed camel column [PLAUSIBLE — Saharan marching norm; exact figure [UNCERTAIN]].
- Officer cadre deliberately forward — sniping them works (Niémilane 25 Oct 1907: 2 off/2 NCO/15 tirailleurs dead; Damané 8 Apr 1908; Liboirat 10 Jan 1913: 60 dead incl. 1 officer — worst loss of the conquest) [VERIFIED — campaign doc].

### 5.4 Post / borj defense
- Square stone **borj** (e.g., Fort Coppolani at Tidjikja), zariba entrenchments for night laagers; a blockhaus corporal + 7 men held off the Gandon rezzou (1857) — small-post heroics are period-accurate [VERIFIED-pattern].
- Moors besiege but rarely storm: no artillery, no siege train [VERIFIED-pattern].

### 5.5 Ambush patterns
- **Palmery assassination**: Coppolani shot in the Tidjikja palmery, 12 May 1905 — cover + intimate knowledge [VERIFIED].
- Waterhole ambushes (Oglat-Saiet 1908) and column-harassment (Niémilane) [VERIFIED — campaign doc].

### 5.6 Siege & negotiation culture
- **Dhahr Tagant "40 horses" norm is PRECOLONIAL, 18th century** (Ahmed ibn Haybah, r. 1728–61, 3-month siege of the Idawish lifted in exchange for 40 horses) — it is the negotiation-culture precedent, NOT a French-era event [VERIFIED — `RESEARCH_precolonial.md`]. Corrects any temptation to date it to the conquest.
- Tribes flip for tribute/protection; sieges end in parley more often than assault [VERIFIED-pattern].

### 5.7 Seasonal rhythm
- **Hivernage** (Jul–Sep rains): French campaigning pauses in the north; south (Chemama) flooded; tsetse active. **Cold season (Oct–Feb)**: peak mobility for both sides — rezzou season and column season. Late dry season: wells shrink, water warfare peaks [VERIFIED-pattern — LOC study seasonal cycle; specific French campaign calendars follow it].
